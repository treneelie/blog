I wanted to learn how to play bass and work with wood, so I built my own five-string bass.

Wow this project was a lot of fun but it was also quite challenging. I had to redo and rethink the design all along the way.

First of all, I would like to thank the EPFL makerspaces, SKIL and SPOT, as well as all the coaches, without whom I would never have been able to do this.

I wanted a five-string bass, even though my friend Constance, who plays the bass, told me it was a bad idea for a beginner (she is right, but I am stubborn). I decided to buy the neck because I acknowledge that I didn't yet have the skills to make it myself. Having never worked with wood, I wanted to start with something that was achievable for me. I decided to go for an active humbucker, the EMG 40DC, rather than a P-Bass or a J-Bass pickup. I chose it mainly for its sound. I don't know that much about pickups, but it seemed like a pretty cool option. The wood I chose was Okoume because it is easy to work with and it is a good wood for instruments.

My first design was inspired by the StingRay 5 bass. I also wanted it to be an alien themed bass. My friend Lou designed the pickguard adapted to the shape of the bass I gave her.

Here is how I wanted the bass to look like. With a little star in the back to change the battery easily. It holds with magnets.

After I had conducted some initial tests by printing the pickguard in two pieces using a single color. My friend Adrian, who had access to a larger printer capable of printing in multiple colors, printed Lou’s design as a single piece:

My first attempt at building the bass was something... First, I created a 3D model in Fusion 360. To accurately model the area where the handle is inserted, I took a photo of the handle's perimeter, traced over it and incorporated it into the 3D model, it works quite well. Then that is where things began to be interesting. I wanted to try doing it with the CNC machine. Well, I didn't in put the right measurements and, because I had to turn the wood over in the process, the backside is offset relative to the top side.

But it’s okay, I managed to fix it all using a wood router, and even gave the design an intentional-looking effect.

After a long session of sanding, I was finally ready to start figuring out how to put everything together. What a mistake not to have checked that beforehand.

I realized that I had made a mistake regarding the length of the bass, and that this was the worst possible error I could have made, as it meant I had to start everything over from scratch. It is impossible to extend the bass, because there is far too much tension in that area due to the strings. Besides, it would look terrible. I’m short by 20mm.

So I breathed. Cried. And then I started again. But this time, I wanted to do it without CNC and learn how to do every step myself and be more vigilent. So I bought some more wood (there goes my money) and cut it up using a bandsaw. To be precise, throughout the project, I frequently used a technique that involved creating a template with a laser cutter, accounting for the offset required for the router’s guide bushing, and then following that outline with the router.

And Bam, my dimensions are correct.

Since I changed the dimensions and shape of my bass, I had to rethink the pickguard. I didn't want to 3D-print it, as I don't find the finish particularly attractive. I considered cutting it out of plexiglass or anodized aluminum, though the latter wouldn't work with the machines I had access to, as it isn't conductive,but eventually, one of the coachs where I was building my bass suggested I use some of his laminated wood. It was perfect, and it’s a beautiful shade of green. To install the pickguard, I drilled holes using a drill press, inserted threaded inserts, and then added some nice-looking screws. Here are some photos of the anodized aluminium I considered, then a photo of laser cut shape to test the dimensions, and finally the final pickguard:

Yes I know, for the pickguard I had no choice but to use the CNC.

To hide the batteries, I changed the star design to planets made of MDF, which I then painted.

Choosing the paints was quite a process, too. In the end, I was advised against using acrylic spray paint, it would have looked bad, and was told to go with polyurethane paint instead. I went to a paint shop, where I was told to go to an auto body shop. The auto body shop then tried to sell us paint that was completely wrong. A coach at Skil had some pink paint and let me try it out, so I ultimately decided to buy the same kind.

I also ran into issues with the bass guitar's tuning machines, as the screws were poor quality and snapped off inside the wood, even though I had drilled pilot holes. I also 3D printed some cylinders to hold the tuning machines firmly in place, since the holes in the neck were too large.
