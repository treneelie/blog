Nixie tubes are awesome

Here I share information about Nixie tubes.

Where to start, I have so much to say about Nixie tubes.

First, what are they? Nixie tubes are a type of vacuum tube display that use a heated filament to emit electrons, which then strike a gas-filled tube to produce light. Usually, the gas is neon, which produces a beautiful orange glow.

They were invented in the 1950s in United States and were widely used in the 1960s and 1970s. Most of the Nixie tubes that can be found come primarily from the USSR, as they were produced in large quantities there.

They are now considered obsolete and are no longer manufactured, but the company Dalibor Farny still make them as a luxury item.

I discovered Nixie tubes in a physics experiment where they were used to display the voltmeter digits. It is not surprising because they were mainly used for the display of numerical information in technical equipment

On my blog you can see that I buid a nixie clock with IN-12a tubes.

Nixie tubes can take different forms and sizes, but also different colors depending on the gas used. Even so, neon is the most common gas used in Nixie tubes. For example this one is made by Dalibor Farny and is filled with argon.

Nixie tubes filled with argon needs more voltage than neon filled nixies tubes and have a much shorter life span.

The tension needed for neon-filled nixie tube is typically around 170-200V DC to strike and to maintain its glow it is around 145-150V DC. For argon-filled nixie tube, the tension needed to strike is around 200-250V DC and to maintain its glow it is around 180-200V DC.

In a nixie tube we have the following elements:

  • Glass envelope
  • Gas
  • Anode
  • Cathodes

How does it work? The nixie tube is a cold cathode gas-discharge tube. When a voltage is applied between the anode and the cathodes, the gas inside the tube ionizes and emits light. Ionization is when electrons from the cathode are released and collide with the gas molecules, causing them to emit light. Each cathode corresponds to a different digit, and when a specific cathode is activated, it lights up the corresponding digit.

The disposition of the cathodes is not as we might expect. They are not arranged from 0 to 9 (for a digit display) and they are typographically designed and shaped for optimal visibility. For exemple, the IN-12 tubes use the arrangement 3 8 9 4 0 5 7 2 6 1 from front (3) to back (1). Russian tubes were made during the intercontinental space race and the growth of domestic use of electronics, so for cost-saving reasons, the 5 and 2 digits were the same form, only upside down.

Neon ions accelerated by the electric field strike the cathodes and can dislodge metal atoms. This phenomenon is called cathode sputtering. This can lead to the deposition of metal on the inner walls of the tube and cause degradation of the cathodes. Black or metallic deposits on glass are often linked to sputtering.Over time, the dislodged metal can form a deposit on the glass, reducing brightness. In certain designs, the controlled addition of mercury vapor helps mitigate this phenomenon, as collisions with mercury atoms alter the energy of the ions bombarding the cathodes. This has made it possible to extend the lifespan of some tubes.
